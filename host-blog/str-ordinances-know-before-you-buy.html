Know Your STR Ordinances Before You Buy Anything

August 2026 · 6 min read

← Back to Host Blog

We bought three different smoke detectors for one property. Not because we made careless choices — because the ordinance changed twice and the product that met every requirement did not exist until two years after it was required. That is the STR ordinance problem in a single sentence.

Before you order a single item for your STR, read your local ordinance. Read your platform requirements. Read your insurance policy. Then read all three again. They are not the same document, they do not always agree, and they change without notice. What was compliant last year may not be compliant today.

Why This Matters More Than Your Thread Count

New hosts spend enormous time picking the perfect sheets and the right shampoo. That stuff matters — we have a full post on it. But compliance is not optional. A non-compliant smoke detector does not just fail inspection — it can void your insurance claim after a fire, expose you to personal liability, and in some jurisdictions trigger fines or permit revocation.

Non-compliance is also invisible until it is not. Guests do not report it. Inspectors catch it. Incidents expose it. By then, you have already had the problem for months or years without knowing.

Three Sources of Requirements — and They Do Not Agree

Most hosts think about ordinance compliance as a single thing. It is not. There are three separate sets of requirements, written by three different parties for three different purposes.

1. Local STR Ordinance

Your county or municipality may require specific smoke detector types, CO detectors, fire extinguishers, emergency exit signage, septic compliance certificates, and more. These are the rules with teeth — fines, permit suspension, and criminal liability in some jurisdictions. They also change. Fannin County, Georgia updated its smoke detector requirements in August 2023 with no direct announcement to existing permit holders. We found out because we stay on top of it. Many hosts did not.

2. Platform Requirements

Airbnb and VRBO have their own mandatory safety requirements that apply to every listing, independent of local law. Airbnb requires a working smoke alarm, working CO alarm, fire extinguisher, and a first aid kit — all accessible to guests. VRBO has similar requirements. These can be more stringent than your local ordinance. They are also enforceable: listings can be delisted for non-compliance.

3. STR Insurance Policy

Your insurance policy has requirements too — and most hosts never read them until they file a claim. If your property has river access, lake access, or a pool, water safety equipment is almost certainly required. Life rings, life jackets, posted signage. If you do not have them and something happens, your claim may be denied. Read the policy. Call your agent and ask them to walk you through the physical safety requirements for your specific property type. Get it in writing.

Host Tip Check all three sources every year. Set a calendar reminder. STR ordinances are actively evolving in most markets — what was compliant last year may not be compliant today.

Our Timeline: Three Smoke Detectors, One Cabin

This is not a hypothetical. This happened across our North Georgia mountain properties. We are sharing it because it illustrates exactly how quickly requirements can change — and how difficult compliance becomes when the market has not caught up.

April 2023
Fannin County STR Ordinance Passes
The ordinance required “smoke detectors” without specifying battery type or connectivity. We installed Alexa-connected detectors with standard AA batteries, which let us receive phone alerts when an alarm triggered. This met the requirement at the time. For a remote property, owner notification felt like the obvious right approach.
August 2023
Ordinance Updated: 10-Year Sealed Lithium Battery Required
The county updated its ordinance to require 10-year sealed lithium batteries in all smoke detectors. Our Alexa-connected AA-battery detectors no longer complied. We searched for a smart detector with a 10-year sealed lithium battery. None existed. We were forced to choose: stay smart with the wrong battery, or go compliant with a dumb detector. We chose compliance and replaced everything with X-Sense detectors — sealed lithium, 10-year, no smart integration. We lost our remote monitoring entirely.
2023–2025
Two Years Without a Compliant Smart Option
For two full years, no manufacturer offered a smart smoke detector with a 10-year sealed lithium battery. We checked every few months. Nothing. Hosts in Fannin County and similar jurisdictions were stuck: you could be smart and non-compliant, or compliant and blind. There was no third option.
August 2025
First Alert SMCO410: The Product That Should Have Existed in 2023
First Alert released the SMCO410 — smart, 10-year sealed lithium, Ring integration. We switched immediately across both properties. The second guest after installation burned a steak in the kitchen. Ring called us and sent a phone notification simultaneously. We called the guests on the cabin landline to confirm everyone was OK while canceling the fire department on a second call. No false alarm charge. No panicked guests. No one drove 45 minutes each way. This is what connected detection looks like when the product finally exists.

The Public Safety Argument for Connected Detectors

A standalone detector that only sounds inside the cabin is designed to save the lives of the people inside. That matters. But a detector that sounds inside a vacant cabin notifies no one. If guests do not hear it, or if the cabin sits empty between stays, there is no alert. No owner notification. No fire department call.

In the North Georgia mountains, a cabin fire does not stay a cabin fire for long. The Chattahoochee National Forest borders many STR properties in the area. By the time a neighbor notices smoke, the fire may have already moved. At that point you are no longer talking about a cabin loss — you are talking about a wildfire threatening other structures, other forest, and other lives.

Connected detectors that alert owners and can trigger emergency response are not a luxury for remote STR properties. They are a public safety tool. The argument for requiring them in STR ordinances is not about protecting host revenue — it is about protecting the community these properties sit in.

A Note to Ordinance Writers

We want to say this directly to anyone involved in writing STR ordinances: check whether compliant products actually exist before you write the requirement.

When Fannin County updated its ordinance in August 2023 to require 10-year sealed lithium batteries, no smart detector with a 10-year sealed lithium battery existed on the market. Hosts were forced to choose between meeting the battery requirement with a dumb detector, or keeping smart monitoring with non-compliant battery technology. The ordinance created a compliance problem with no correct answer because the product the ordinance implied did not yet exist.

The First Alert SMCO410 arrived in August 2025 — two full years after the requirement was written. For two years, every host in Fannin County who wanted to be both compliant and connected was out of options through no fault of their own. Ordinance writers owe it to the hosts they regulate, and to the communities they protect, to verify that the market can actually supply what they mandate. Talk to hosts before you finalize requirements. A 30-minute conversation with working hosts could have prevented two years of this problem.

Get Involved Before the Rules Are Written Against You

The best way to fix bad ordinances is to be in the room when they are written. STR owners are stakeholders in these decisions. You own property in the county. You pay taxes. You employ local cleaners, vendors, and tradespeople. You bring visitors who eat at local restaurants, buy groceries at local stores, and fill local gas stations. You have standing. Use it.

Attend county commission meetings when STR items are on the agenda. Join your local STR association if one exists. If one does not exist, start one. When ordinance changes are proposed, show up and speak. Bring data. Bring product research. Bring the perspective of someone who has to actually comply with what gets written. One organized group of hosts speaking at a public comment session carries more weight than dozens of individual complaints filed after the fact.

Ordinances written without host input are ordinances written against hosts. The people in the room when the rules are made are the people the rules get written for. If you are not in the room, you are not at the table — you are on the menu.

Compliance Checklist: Start Here

This is a starting framework, not a complete list. Every jurisdiction is different. Verify against your specific county ordinance, platform requirements, and insurance policy.

  • Smoke detector — verify battery type, connectivity requirements, and placement for your jurisdiction
  • CO detector — required in most STR jurisdictions and by both Airbnb and VRBO
  • Fire extinguisher — kitchen placement, check pressure gauge at every turnover
  • First aid kit — visible and accessible to guests (Airbnb requirement)
  • Emergency exit information — posted in unit where required
  • Water safety equipment — life ring, life jackets, posted signage if water access is present (insurance requirement)
  • STR operating permit — posted or on file as required by your jurisdiction
  • Occupancy limits — documented in listing and in house rules
  • Noise ordinance hours — posted in unit and in house manual
  • Septic compliance — some counties require certification for STR permit renewal
Host Tip Pull your county’s STR ordinance document and your platform’s host safety requirements side by side. Where they conflict, apply the stricter standard. Where they agree, verify you actually have the product installed. Do this annually.
