How to Dissuade Bad Guests from Booking

August 2026 · 10 min read

← Back to Host Blog

The right guests make hosting effortless. The wrong ones can cost you thousands of dollars, a string of bad reviews, and weeks of stress. The good news: most bad guest situations are preventable. Not with luck, but with a system.

This is ours. Everything here is something we actually do across our properties in the North Georgia mountains. None of it is theory.

1. Replace Keyed Locks with a Smart Deadbolt

The first thing a bad guest looks for is how to get back in after checkout, or how to let in people you never approved. A physical key solves neither problem. A Schlage Encode smart deadbolt does.

Generate a unique code for each booking and expire it automatically at checkout. No key copies, no locksmith calls, no wondering who still has access. You control entry from your phone. We cover the exact model we use and how we set it up in our STR supplies post.

One important note: the Schlage Encode is sometimes marketed as Airbnb compatible, meaning you can grant Airbnb permission to manage your lock. Do not do this. Your lock controls who enters your property. Keep that control with you, not with a platform.

Still keep a lockbox on the property. You will need it when the batteries die or a guest cannot figure out the keypad.

2. Cover Every Entrance with Cameras

Install at least two cameras per entrance, one solar and one plug-in, mounted from different directions covering the same entry point. Here is why both matter:

  • The plug-in does not record when power is lost.
  • The solar records during a power outage, and if your internet equipment is on a UPS and still running, you can watch those recordings in real time.
  • Link them so when one detects motion, both record.

The specific camera models and mounting strategy we use are covered in our STR supplies post. Most importantly: point each camera at another camera. We recently had guests attempt to turn off every single camera on the property. We caught the whole thing because each camera was watching the one they just disabled. They could not get them all.

Host Tip Disclose your cameras in your listing. Airbnb requires it, and guests who intend to cause problems will sometimes self-select out when they see cameras disclosed upfront. That is the point.

3. Set Up Your Listing to Filter Out Bad Actors

Your listing settings and house rules are your first line of defense. Most hosts underuse both.

Settings

  • Turn off Instant Booking. Every request should require your approval before it is confirmed.
  • Disclose cameras. Required by Airbnb and a deterrent on its own.
  • Set a maximum occupancy. If you do not set it, guests will push past it.
  • Enable an extra person fee. It creates a financial consequence for exceeding occupancy and gives you documentation if they do.

House Rules

Do not write vague rules. Write rules with teeth. Here is the language we use and recommend:

Sample House Rules

  • Only registered guests are allowed on the property at any time.
  • All guests must be registered prior to arrival.
  • No locals. Booking guest must be traveling from outside the area.
  • Booking guest must be at least [age] years old and present during the entire stay.
  • Each guest is required to provide a government-issued ID with current address.
  • Booking guest must message host with the total number of guests, including infants, when submitting a request to book.
  • Guests must respond to all host messages within [X] hours using the platform.
  • If maximum occupancy is exceeded, host will charge extra person fees or cancel the stay at the host’s discretion. If guests do not vacate immediately following cancellation, law enforcement will be notified that trespassers are on the property.
  • No sleeping on the couch.
  • Host or host’s agent may enter during a stay to investigate, document, troubleshoot, and/or resolve issues detected by monitoring systems, reported by others, or reported by the guest. Stay may be canceled if an issue requires immediate mitigation to prevent further damage.
  • Guest will pay replacement value for anything they damage.
  • Dogs are welcome. No other animals allowed.

4. Take Date and Time Stamped Photos After Every Clean

Document the condition of your property after every turnover, before the next guest arrives. Enable the date and time watermark in your phone camera settings. This takes 30 seconds to set up and gives you ironclad documentation if a guest disputes damage or claims something was already broken.

Without timestamped photos, it is your word against theirs. With them, it is not.

5. Get the Headcount in Writing Before You Accept

Before you accept any booking request, require the guest to confirm in writing the total number of humans who will be on the property — adults, children, and infants included. Do not rely on what Airbnb shows you. The platform does not surface the full picture.

Include an explanation in your message: “While you may have already provided some of this information to Airbnb, Airbnb does not share all of it with us as your hosts. Therefore, we require guests to message it directly to us.” This tells the guest why you are asking without making them feel accused, and it puts the platform’s information gap on the record before you accept.

Do not accept a request until you have that answer in writing on the platform. If they do not respond, you do not accept. The headcount in writing is your documentation if they show up with more people than they declared. Without it, you have nothing to stand on.

Host Tip: Airbnb Does Not Count Infants in Your Guest Count When a guest enters 2 adults, 1 child, and 1 infant on Airbnb, the platform shows you a guest count of 3. The infant is not counted. You accepted what you thought was a party of 3. Four people show up. This is not a mistake — it is how Airbnb calculates guest count, and it catches hosts off guard constantly. Asking guests to confirm the breakdown and the total — directly on the platform, before you accept — is the only way to know what is actually coming through your door. 6. Install AirReview Before You Accept Another Booking

AirReview is a free Chrome extension by Rankbreeze that lets you read the reviews a guest has left for prior hosts directly on their Airbnb profile. Install it on your desktop browser. It takes two minutes.

This is information Airbnb does not surface prominently. AirReview puts it front and center.

7. Read the Reviews They Left for Prior Hosts

A guest who left a prior host less than five stars is a guest you should decline. Full stop. It does not matter what the prior host wrote about them in their review. If the guest thought that stay deserved less than five stars, they will find a reason to give you less than five stars too.

8. Check Their Trip Count and Average Stars Received

Look at how many trips the guest has taken and what their average star rating from prior hosts is. If their average is less than five stars, decline. Even if every prior host wrote something positive, the star rating is the honest signal. Prior hosts who gave glowing written reviews but fewer than five stars were being polite. Trust the number.

9. Collect Government-Issued ID

Require every guest to provide a government-issued ID with their current address as part of the booking process. State this clearly in your house rules. Guests who are unwilling to verify their identity before accessing your property are guests worth declining.

Host Tip None of these steps will stop every problem. But together they make your property a significantly harder target than the listing next door that has none of them. Most bad actors look for the path of least resistance. Make sure that path does not lead to your cabin.
