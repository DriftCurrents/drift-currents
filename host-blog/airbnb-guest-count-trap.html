Know Your Platform

Know Your OTA: The Airbnb Baby Got Booked Trap

August 2026 · 5 min read

← Back to Host Blog

A guest enters 2 adults, 1 child, and 1 infant on Airbnb. Your inbox shows a guest count of 3. You accept. Four people show up.

You did not make a mistake. This is how Airbnb’s platform calculates guest count. Infants are not counted in the guest count Airbnb displays to hosts. This is not a bug. It is how the platform works — and most hosts find out the hard way.

How Airbnb Counts Guests Why It Matters Occupancy and Extra Fees The Fix Platform Literacy

How Airbnb Counts Guests

When a guest submits a booking request on Airbnb, they enter their party breakdown: adults, children, and infants separately. Airbnb combines adults and children into the guest count it shows hosts. Infants are tracked separately by the platform but are not included in the number that appears in your reservation inbox.

Example

What the guest enters: 2 adults · 1 child · 1 infant

What Airbnb shows you: 3 guests

What actually shows up: 4 people

The infant is in the reservation. Airbnb knows about the infant. You, as the host, are not shown the infant in the primary guest count displayed in your inbox.

There is an additional layer: Airbnb does not require guests to disclose that they are bringing an infant. A guest can complete the booking without mentioning the infant at all, and Airbnb will not flag it as a disclosure problem. The platform’s own help documentation confirms that hosts cannot decline a reservation solely because a guest did not disclose they are traveling with children.

What Airbnb’s Policy Actually Says Per Airbnb’s help center: children are subject to a host’s maximum guest capacity — meaning hosts can decline if the total number of guests including children exceeds their stated maximum. But hosts cannot decline a reservation because a guest did not disclose they were traveling with children. The platform puts the burden of knowing and asking on you. Sources: Host families with children · Traveling with children

Why It Matters

A Common Host Belief: “Infants Don’t Count.” This belief likely comes from the platform itself. Airbnb excludes infants from the guest count it shows hosts, and many hosts have interpreted that as a policy position. It is not. Airbnb’s own help documentation states that children are subject to a host’s maximum guest capacity. What the platform displays and what the policy allows are two different things. A host running their property on the assumption that infants don’t count may be accepting reservations that exceed their permitted occupancy without knowing it.

Your maximum occupancy is a number you set for a reason. It reflects your property’s capacity, your insurance policy’s requirements, and your local ordinance’s limits. “2 guests maximum” means 2 humans — not 2 humans and however many infants Airbnb doesn’t surface to the host and the guest may not know they need to disclose.

If your property sleeps 3 and a guest shows up with 2 adults, a toddler, and an infant, you now have 4 people in a space permitted for 3. If something happens — a safety incident, a noise complaint, an insurance claim — you accepted a reservation where the actual party size exceeded your stated limit. You did so because Airbnb showed you a number that was not the full picture.

Local fire marshals and code enforcement officers do not use Airbnb’s guest count. They count heads. Your local ordinance sets occupancy based on people in the structure — not platform-calculated guests. An infant in a property over its permitted occupancy is still an occupancy violation. The platform’s classification does not change what local law requires.

This is not a hypothetical concern. It is a real platform behavior that creates a gap between what you agreed to and what actually arrives at your door.

That gap has a business cost. A host who discovers the undisclosed infant at check-in is now in an awkward position: enforce your rules and risk the guest feeling accused of something they may not have understood they needed to disclose, or say nothing and absorb the occupancy overage. Either way, friction has entered the stay before it has started. Friction leads to bad reviews. A four-star review with a comment about a “strict host” costs you future bookings.

The platform, meanwhile, benefits from a family-friendly reputation. Marketing to guests with children drives bookings. The host absorbs the friction and the review risk. The platform absorbs neither.

Occupancy Limits, Extra Person Fees, and What Airbnb Enforces

Airbnb allows hosts to set a maximum occupancy and an extra person fee above a base guest count. The platform applies these settings to the guest count it calculates — which, as covered above, excludes infants.

This means: if your extra person fee kicks in at 2 guests, and a guest books for 2 adults and 1 infant, Airbnb may not trigger the extra person fee because it sees 2 guests, not 3 people. Your fee structure is applied to an incomplete count.

It also means that if your occupancy limit is 2 and a guest books for 2 adults and 1 infant, Airbnb may approve the booking as within your occupancy limit — because it sees 2 guests.

On fees specifically: Airbnb’s own help documentation states that whether to charge for infants under 2 is host discretion. That means you can write your house rules and extra person fee structure to include infants — but only if you explicitly do so. If your rules and fee settings don’t mention infants, the platform has no basis to charge for them. The default works against you. See: Airbnb guest limits.

The Gap Airbnb’s occupancy enforcement is based on the guest count it calculates. Infants are excluded from that calculation. Your house rules say maximum 2. Airbnb sees 2. Three people show up. From the platform’s perspective, the guest complied.

The Fix: Require the Breakdown in Writing Before You Accept

Airbnb’s platform behavior is not going to change based on what you prefer. Your protection has to come from your own process.

Before accepting any booking request, require the guest to confirm in writing the total number of humans in their party — including adults, children, and infants. Not a platform count. Not a guest count. Every human who will be on the property.

Do not accept the request until you have that message. When it arrives, you have written documentation — on the Airbnb platform, timestamped — of exactly what the guest told you their party was. If more people show up than were declared, you have a record of the discrepancy that pre-dates check-in.

Your house rules should state maximum occupancy in terms that include infants explicitly. “Maximum 3 humans including adults, children, and infants” leaves no ambiguity. “Maximum 3 guests” leaves the door open for exactly the interpretation Airbnb’s platform encourages.

What to Ask — and Why to Say It Message every guest before accepting and ask them to confirm the total number of humans who will be on the property, including adults, children, and infants. Then explain why: “While you may have already provided some of this information to Airbnb, Airbnb does not share all of it with us as your hosts. Therefore, we require guests to message it directly to us.” That sentence explains the ask without making the guest feel accused, and it puts the platform’s information gap on the record — in writing, on the platform, before you accept.

This Is a Platform Literacy Problem

The guest count issue is one example of a broader pattern: Airbnb’s platform makes decisions about how information is presented to hosts, and those decisions are not always in your interest as a host. The platform is designed primarily for the guest experience. Your protection as a host requires you to understand where the gaps are and build your own processes to fill them.

The guest count trap is not the only one. Airbnb no longer shares real guest contact information with hosts at all. The platform now routes communication through fake temporary phone numbers provided to both hosts and guests. That means if a guest leaves their favorite bathing suit at your cabin and does not discover it for two weeks, they cannot call you. You cannot call them. The contact channel the platform controls disappears, and neither side has a direct line to the other.

The information asymmetry runs deeper than phone numbers. Whether Airbnb verifies that guests use their real legal names and photos in their profiles is a separate question — and not one the platform answers clearly for hosts. You are accepting strangers into your property based on a profile you cannot fully verify, through a contact channel the platform controls, with a guest count that may not reflect everyone who shows up.

Reading the TOS is not enough. You need to understand how the platform actually behaves in practice — where its calculations differ from what you would expect, what information it withholds, and where your house rules and pre-booking process need to compensate. That is platform literacy. It is as important as knowing your local ordinance, and most hosts never develop it until something goes wrong.
